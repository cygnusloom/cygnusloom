Cygnus Loom is an RPG engine that uses an AI game master to run the game, with a comprehensive engine to ensure the AI GM plays the game correctly, follows the rules, and keeps track of everything.

# Campaigns, Rulesets, and Worlds
A campaign is a running game instance. It consists of an event log tracking everything that happens - this is the source of truth. Each campaign has a ruleset, which determins how the AI GM can act, how things behave, when dice are used, and so on. A campaign is also based on a world, which is the template for setting up the game world for the player and AI GM to play in.

A campaign belongs to one account, its owner. The owner can share it with other accounts through join links. Each player makes their own player character, and the AI GM sees every player character at the table and brings new ones into the story. Out of combat any player can move the story forward; in an encounter a player acts for their own character on that character's turn or through a feature reaction. Every player's page follows the campaign live, so a turn started by any player updates the whole table. Players are known to each other by a display name they choose; account email addresses stay private.

# AI Game Master - Cygnus
Cygnus is the AI GM. Cygnus will keep the game on track, following all the rules and not allowing any inconsistency. However, Cygnus understands that the story is for the players, so if the players insist, Cygnus will change the story or scene to better fit what story the players want to tell.

# Player Dice
Whenever the player needs to roll some dice, the game will stop and let the player choose to roll their dice (prompted accordingly) or enter a free-form response. This is equivalent to a real game master asking their player to roll the dice, and the player can either roll the dice or respond to the GM in another way.

# Encounters
Encounters are scenes within the game where simulated time slows down so actions can be resolved according to the ruleset, usually for combat. Actors in encounters will often take turns. The turn order comes from the rule set's initiative, and each rule set keeps its own source material's order: a side-initiative rule set keeps each side together in the order its roll set, while a rule set that checks initiative per character keeps that order. The AI GM can adjust the order or whose turn is running only when a rule or the story calls for an exception, such as a held action, a side choosing its order, or a correction; in ordinary play the order stands. The player clicks on a button to start an NPC's turn, and the AI GM runs it. The player's own turn starts automatically when it comes up; they click on a button to end it. The turn checklist is a set of reminders for the AI GM to consider every area, and an encounter turn adds reminders for the acting character's attacks, movement, and other turn resources. The ruleset can measure attacks on the encounter grid: melee reach, ranged bands with their hit penalties, and the encounter objects that block a line of sight. A weapon or statblock attack carries its own reach or ranges; an attack with neither is melee at the ruleset's default reach, which is the next cell unless the ruleset gives a distance. A ranged band may have no limit, written as a distance of 0, reaching any target the line of sight allows, so a rule set like Cairn's can let a bow shoot across the whole map while keeping melee to one cell. The engine refuses an attack that is out of reach or range, or whose line of sight is blocked, and the GM moves the attacker before trying again.

# Music
Each player has background music, and the AI GM chooses its mood for the scene. The GM never picks a track: the browser chooses a licensed track that matches the mood, crossfades it, and remembers the player's mute and volume. Combat starts and stops automatically, and a party with no character left in play falls to sorrow. The GM overrides the derived mood when the story needs something else. Music is bundled with the app, so it works offline and never sends a request to a third-party music service. A browser holds music until the player's first click, tap, or key press; after that, music falls silent while the window is in the background and returns when the player comes back, in both the browser and the desktop edition.

# Generic Engine, JSON rules
The engine should support various rulesets. It should not be a hodgepodge of spaghetti code as new rulesets pile on. Instead, ruleset behavior should be defined in the ruleset JSON. The engine itself must remain clean. Use modular systems with hooks, parameters, and customizable behavior so each ruleset can define how the engine behaves. If custom code is absolutely necessary, it can be contained within each ruleset's python module. However, most RPG concepts are similar enough that they can live within the generic core engine and just be parameterized in the JSON ruleset.

# Unit tests
The game has many unit tests. These must be regularly optimized and evaluated, as they can take a significant amount of time to run. They should also be evaluated to ensure that useless unit tests are removed or never written.
